You are right about the literature, and more strongly than the point usually gets made

Argued about trials, your position is contestable. Argued about preclinical work it is close to unanswerable, and preclinical work is what the peptide evidence base actually is. Amgen replicated six of fifty-three landmark cancer papers, some with the original authors present and blinded. Bayer validated about a quarter. The paper your team should be citing and is not

RCT-DUPLICATE, in JAMA in 2023. Pre-registered protocols, thirty-two randomised trials, reproduced using ordinary claims data. Overall correlation with the trials was 0.82. In the sixteen where the trial could be emulated closely, it was 0.93, and agreement on statistical significance went from three quarters to ninety-four per cent.

Read it the way your mathematicians would. The databases were the same size in both states. Nothing about volume changed. What changed was whether the analysis could reproduce the trial's eligibility window, its starting point, its comparator and its outcome definition. That is the strongest published support your position has, and what it identifies as the lever is design discipline rather than scale.

Where the same literature turns on you

In the Ioannidis paper everyone quotes for the thirty-two per cent, there is a split that rarely gets quoted. Five of six highly cited nonrandomised studies were contradicted or found to have been exaggerated, against nine of thirty-nine randomised trials. Eighty-three per cent against twenty-three.

The first contradicted finding it lists is the Nurses' Health Study: an enormous real-world cohort of women using hormone therapy exactly as they chose, reporting a forty-four per cent reduction in coronary events. The Women's Health Initiative randomised it and found increased risk. Its own observational arm agreed with the cohort and disagreed with its own trial arm, on the same women. The cause was that women who chose hormones were healthier before they started, and no additional women would have revealed that. The same pattern took down beta-carotene, vitamin E, folate and multivitamins.

That is the objection Ēnel specifically has to answer, because the people enrolling in a peptide study are the healthy-user population in its purest available form. Affluent, training, sleeping, tracking, self-selected on exactly the traits that predict good outcomes. Whatever you measure will look like it worked.

The one place I will not move

More data buys precision. It does not buy validity. Bias does not shrink with n. A confounded effect measured on a million people is the same wrong number as on a thousand, reported with more confidence, and that is worse rather than better, because a narrow interval around a wrong answer looks like proof and gets quoted by every brand in the category.

What volume genuinely buys is the ability to cut many trustworthy comparisons out of one cohort. That is still an argument for your massive study. It is a different argument from the one being made.

So build the thing you described, and collect for emulation

Your own thesis already names target trial emulation as the rigour anchor, which is exactly what RCT-DUPLICATE measures. It asks nothing extra of the participant and a great deal of the intake, and most of it cannot be added later. The exact date someone started. What they were doing in the weeks before. Whether this is their first ever course. A comparator drawn from inside the population rather than outside it, which is a technical argument for all-people. Then negative controls and a published sensitivity figure beside every estimate.

And lead with the five things no trial can answer: why people stop and whether it faded or never worked, what doses and stacks people actually run, harms at scale in the exposed population, where they bought it, and who never responds. Those are unimpeachable, they are yours alone, and publishing them first makes the first attack on the method much harder to land.

The framing I would fight you on

Drop "very little respect for studies done historically," not because it is unfair but because it costs you the docket. FDA's reviewers are the audience, and a submission that reads as a rejection of prior research gets filed and ignored whatever is in it. Your thesis already has the better line: prior art is a quarry rather than a foundation. That concedes nothing and cannot be quoted back at you.

One gap worth naming. Adams is maths and statistics, Shakeel is machine learning, Dickinson and Overcash are clinical. Causal inference from observational data is its own discipline, and the person who writes an emulation protocol and a bias analysis is a pharmacoepidemiologist.
