01 · The answer, stated first

Open on the six peptides the FDA is about to rule on, and publish into the rulemaking.

Your instinct on peptides is correct, and the reason is stronger than the reason you have been giving. It is not that the category is large. Measured as a market it is small, somewhere around two to three billion dollars against a supplement market of seventy-three billion. It is that on 24 July 2026 an FDA advisory committee voted to let compounding pharmacies make six of these compounds while the agency's own reviewers had written, in the briefing documents for that same meeting, that there is a lack of evidence to evaluate their effectiveness and safety.

That sentence is the whole opportunity. A federal agency has said in writing that the evidence does not exist. Its advisers overrode it. Formal rulemaking now has to happen, which means a proposed rule, a public comment docket and a final rule, on a clock that these processes usually run in eight to twenty-four months. Somebody is going to put evidence into that docket. Today nobody has any. There is no registry of BPC-157 users anywhere, and as of March 2026 there were no trials of it even recruiting.

So the opening is not a splash for its own sake. It is the one move that puts Ēnel's name inside a federal process within twelve months, on a budget the first $450,000 already covers, in a position no competitor can buy because it goes to whoever files first with real data.

The recommendation in five lines

  1. Study one is the PCAC six, which is BPC-157, TB-500, KPV, MOTS-c, Semax and Epitalon, observed in people already taking them.
  2. Strictly observational. You record the protocol somebody is already running. You never set one, never supply anything, never advise a dose.
  3. Roughly a thousand people, not a mass enrolment. The audience is trivially available and the constraint was never supply.
  4. Run a paying register underneath it in three ordinary supplement categories, because peptides will not pay you and supplements will.
  5. Bring creators in as subjects once one study has published, not before, and give them a process mark rather than an efficacy mark.

What follows is why, where I disagree with the shape you described, five other openings I considered and rejected or deferred, and the specific things that have to be written down before any of it can start.

03 · Three words that have to change

All-peptides, all-people, all-protocols

Each of those three is doing damage, and one of them is a legal problem rather than a design preference.

All-protocols is the dangerous one

If Ēnel assigns a protocol, recommends a dose, supplies a compound or tells a participant to change what they are doing, the study stops being observational and becomes a clinical investigation of an unapproved drug. The usual exemption from filing an investigational new drug application is available only for a drug that is lawfully marketed in the United States. These peptides are not lawfully marketed, so the exemption is unavailable and the exposure is real. An institutional review board does not cure it. Boards approve human-subjects protection; they do not grant relief from drug law.

The workable version is narrow and it is still enormously valuable. You record the protocol the person is already running, in their own words, with the dose they chose, from the source they used, for as long as they keep going. You add nothing to their behaviour and you take nothing away from it. That design is publishable, insurable, and defensible in front of a regulator, and it is the only version that can also be filed into a docket without becoming the story for the wrong reason.

The line to write into the protocol, and never cross

Ēnel observes. Ēnel does not prescribe, dispense, supply, dose, titrate, recommend, or advise cessation. Every participant arrives with a regimen they chose before Ēnel existed in their life, and leaves with the same one unless their own clinician changes it.

All-peptides dilutes the finding to nothing

A hundred and twenty-nine compounds, times the number of protocols people actually run, times the number of stacks they run them in, produces a cell count that will never reach a publishable conclusion anywhere. The first study needs few enough compounds that each one gets a real n and an endpoint somebody can check.

The six the committee voted on are not an arbitrary six. They are the ones with a federal process attached, which means the scope of study one has a justification that is nothing to do with commercial interest. That is a rare and valuable thing to be able to say about a first study, and it answers the question the council spent most of its time on, which is who chose what gets measured and why.

All-people is right for the platform and wrong for study one

An open cohort of everybody gives you volume without comparability. What makes a finding land is a population where a comparator already exists inside the cohort. The obvious one here is the overlap you are already sitting on. Eleven per cent of US adults are currently on a GLP-1 and fifteen per cent have used one, and a meaningful share of those people are adding a second compound for muscle preservation, recovery, hair or sleep. Those people can be compared with GLP-1 users who added nothing, inside the same cohort, on the same measurements. That is a real study with a real control group and it costs nothing extra to design.

Massive is a false economy

A big study here is about a thousand people. That number is already in the model and it is the number that should be in the press release. Massive and vague costs the same as focused and defensible, takes longer to fill, and publishes less. Given a community of two hundred thousand on one forum alone and ten million searches a month, recruitment is not the constraint and never was. Protocol design, adjudication and the publication gates are the constraint, which is the honest thing to say and the thing that makes the operating plan credible.

04 · Six openings

What else I considered

You asked for viewpoints rather than a verdict, so here are the six I worked through, including the two I would not do and the one I think is the quiet hedge worth four phone calls.

A

Peptides, as you described it

All compounds, all people, all protocols, one very large IRB study, launched as a splash.
The case
Maximum surface area, maximum press, and the library already covers the whole field. One study to point at instead of twelve.
What breaks it
All-protocols invites a drug-law problem the IRB cannot solve. All-compounds guarantees that no single finding reaches significance. The splash arrives before there is anything to report, which spends the attention at the moment you have least to say.
ReviseRight category, wrong three adjectives.
B

GLP-1 first

Go where the mass actually is. Eleven per cent of adults currently using, fifteen per cent ever, awareness at ninety-one per cent.
The case
The largest live question in consumer health, the loudest social conversation, and a population already measuring itself weekly.
What breaks it
The interesting efficacy questions belong to Novo and Lilly and are already answered by registrational trials. FDA issued thirty warning letters in March 2026 and twenty-five more in June to telehealth firms over compounded GLP-1 claims, so the adjacent commercial space is under active enforcement. You would be a small voice in a crowded, well-funded room.
Fold inUse it as the comparator arm and the recruiting channel, not the headline.
C

The docket wedge

The six PCAC compounds, observed, plus the question nobody is asking, which is what is actually in the vial people bought.
The case
A named federal process with a comment period and no evidence in it. No registry exists. No trials were recruiting as of March 2026. Ēnel's library already covers every compound and can show where the evidence stops.
What breaks it
FDA is not obliged to follow its committee and could revert, which would strand a study designed only for the docket. Mitigated by designing it to stand on its own as the first real-world safety record in the category.
Lead with thisThe one opening that cannot be bought by a better-funded competitor.
D

Register first, on ordinary supplements

Skip the splash. Open the master protocol in three high-prevalence categories where brands already have budget to protect.
The case
This is where the money is. Validation and listing is the largest line in the model at $267M, and supplement brands spend thirty to forty per cent of revenue on marketing while carrying a twenty to sixty per cent penalty on cost per lead for want of substantiation.
What breaks it
Nothing breaks it. It is simply not a story, and a company with fifteen new members a week needs a story as much as it needs a customer.
Run underneathThe revenue beachhead, deliberately separate from the attention beachhead.
E

Creator cohort first

Make study one the influencer study. Recruit the people with audiences and let their reach do the launch.
The case
Fastest reach available, no paid channel required, and it is the arrangement with a creator that does not contradict the decision to displace them.
What breaks it
If the first thing Ēnel ever publishes is about famous people, the method is judged by the company it keeps. You also have no track record to point to when a creator asks what the finding will look like, which is the moment the offer either lands or does not.
Second actPowerful, and it needs one published study behind it first.
F

Panel signal to payers and employers

Sell population-level visibility of what people take outside the formulary, to plans and self-insured employers.
The case
It prices the panel rather than the verdict, so the conflict that dogs every other line disappears entirely. Payers currently have no view of this and carry the cost when it interacts badly.
What breaks it
Long sales cycles, procurement, and nothing to show a consumer. It cannot be a beachhead because it produces no audience.
Test now, cheaplyFour conversations before the raise opens tells you whether a conflict-free second side exists.

Two openings I did not put on the list, and should say why. Clinician-directed advertising and clinical trial recruitment are both larger than anything above, and both are excluded by the scope you set. They stay on the shelf until the record is deep enough to make either of them a two-year build rather than a five-year one.

05 · Where the money actually is

In peptides, the buyer is the channel, not the brand

This is the part of the peptide plan I would change most, and it is the part most likely to be caught by a good investor in the meeting rather than after it.

The register earns its money from brands who exist as brands, can legally advertise efficacy, and have a marketing budget to defend. In peptides, none of that holds. The sellers are compounding pharmacies, licensed telehealth practices, and a long tail of research-chemical resellers. Efficacy claims are not available to any of them, because the compounds are unapproved and, right now, not even lawful to compound. So if the plan implies peptide validation revenue, the plan is wrong, and the correction is easy to make now and awkward to make later.

What peptides earn you is the record, the audience, the standing and a federal citation. What they can also earn, and this is the piece I have not seen written down anywhere in the materials, is money from the legitimate channel trying to distinguish itself from the grey market.

The finding nobody has and everybody needs

Nobody knows what is in the vials people are buying. Grey-market peptides are widely reported to carry wrong doses, contaminants, or different substances altogether. If Ēnel records where each participant sourced their compound, and later tests a sample of what they hold, it becomes the only source of truth on supply-side quality in the category.

That finding harms no legitimate seller. It helps every consumer immediately. It is precisely what a rulemaking docket needs. And it gives licensed pharmacies and telehealth prescribers a commercial reason to fund the record, because the finding is the thing that separates them from a reseller with a website. That is the peptide-category customer, and it is a customer who wants the study to be rigorous rather than favourable.

The substantiation standard is a design requirement

Related, and load-bearing for the largest line in the model. The FTC's Health Products Compliance Guidance says that health-benefit claims will generally need randomised, controlled human trials to meet the competent-and-reliable-evidence standard, and that epidemiological evidence substitutes only where experts in the field accept it and randomised trials are not feasible.

Read against the register, that says an observational score on its own does not let a brand make a claim. Which means the thing a brand pays for cannot be the observation. It has to be the randomised arm nested inside the open cohort, which the model already prices, and that arm has to actually randomise. The good news is that this also resolves the sharpest objection on the council record. A paying brand gets a randomised arm and a claim it can use. Everybody else in the category gets the descriptive finding, published, for free. The difference between the two is legible, defensible, and has nothing to do with the verdict being for sale.

06 · The generation, read honestly

Right target, and one number that should stop you overclaiming

Gen Z and millennials are the correct audience and the evidence is strong. They are thirty-six per cent of US adults and forty-one per cent of annual wellness spend. Close to thirty per cent say they are prioritising wellness a lot more than a year ago, against as little as twenty-three per cent of older groups. Three quarters of Gen Z reach for AI before professional care. They trust independent research and reviews above brands, and ninety-four per cent of brands score lower on trust with them than with anyone else.

Here is the number that complicates the story, and I would rather you heard it from me than from an investor. Asked what they plan to do about their health, only about twenty-one per cent of this group say they intend to optimise or biohack with technology, tracking or specialised supplements. Seventy-seven per cent say they intend to simplify, around sleep, hydration and exercise.

That does not damage the beachhead. A beachhead is supposed to be a small, intense population, and the twenty-one per cent are exactly the people who self-experiment, buy the compounds, keep the spreadsheets and post the results. It damages the sizing. If a deck uses the forty-one per cent of wellness spend to imply that the whole generation is the peptide market, the arithmetic falls apart under one question. Use the loud minority to open, and the simplifiers to scale later through sleep, gut and the ordinary categories where they actually spend.

The people who will make the first study are not the generation. They are the fraction of it that already runs experiments on itself and publishes the results for nothing but attention.

Which is the point worth keeping in front of every investor conversation. Millions of people already publish their health story for free, in exchange for attention alone. Ēnel is not asking for a new behaviour. It is offering the same behaviour somewhere it is reciprocated, measured against their own baseline, and accumulated into something that outlasts the post.

07 · The creators

Right idea, wrong verb

Migration is the wrong frame. You do not want creators to leave TikTok and Instagram, and they will not. Their audience is the asset they bring, and it lives there. What you want is for them to keep the audience where it is and bring it here to watch a result get settled. Ēnel is not a competitor to those platforms. It is the laboratory they have never had.

The timing argument is genuinely good, and better than the one in the deck. Verified AI-generated influencers grew more than forty per cent in 2025 and are projected to take around fifteen per cent of influencer marketing spend by the end of 2026. Consumer preference for AI-made creator content has fallen to about twenty-six per cent from sixty per cent in 2023. Human creators are being crowded by synthetic competition at the exact moment their audiences say they want the human thing. What they lack is any way to prove they are both real and right.

Ēnel can issue that proof. Two rules make it work and both of them protect you.

Sign before the result is known

A creator agrees to be studied, and agrees to publication, before anybody knows the answer. Nothing is paid against the outcome, which is already the standing rule. This is the whole integrity of the arrangement, and it is also what makes the content good. An audience can tell the difference between somebody who was tested and somebody who was sponsored.

A process mark, never an efficacy mark

What a creator may display is that they were studied here and that the finding, whatever it was, is published here. Not a score, not a badge implying the product works. A mark in consumer-facing advertising is worth what the certifier's reputation is worth, and Ēnel will not have that standing for three to five years. Handing an efficacy mark to a creator before then puts the most valuable thing you own in the hands of the person with the least incentive to protect it.

And the refusals are useful. Somebody with a large audience who declines to have their claim tested has told that audience something, and you never have to say a word about it.

09 · Before any of it

Five things to write down first

The council record landed on one of these already and I would not soften it. The rest follow from the peptide plan specifically.

  1. The study selection rule. Who chooses which category is measured, in what order, against what comparator, at what dose, over what window, at what slot price, and who decides when the rule and the money disagree. The peptide opening makes this urgent rather than theoretical, because the first sponsored offer in this category will come from the largest seller in it, and it will arrive with a number attached and nothing objectionable on its face.
  2. The observational boundary. One paragraph, in the protocol and in the consent, stating that Ēnel does not prescribe, supply, dose or advise. This is what keeps a registry a registry.
  3. The IRB relationship. Wanted for publication and partnership anyway, required for the research exemption under Washington's My Health My Data Act and Nevada SB 370, and a precondition for a Certificate of Confidentiality. Nothing about a peptide cohort should be enrolled before it exists.
  4. Consent written for third-party studies now. Retrofitting consent across an enrolled panel is not possible, and every week of enrolment under narrow consent shrinks what can be licensed later.
  5. What the register is, in one page. Name each asset separately, name who pays for which, and name what a paying brand receives that the non-paying brands in its category do not. On the model above the answer is a randomised arm and a usable claim, which is a clean answer, and it is currently not written anywhere.

Not legal advice. The drug-law, FTC and state-privacy points here need counsel's sign-off before a single participant is enrolled, and the IND question in particular should go to a regulatory lawyer rather than a general one.
